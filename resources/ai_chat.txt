                        3. Why did you make those changes?
                            The core problem was that the original code did no pruning at all — it couldn't reject a bad partial path until it was fully completed. 
                            Every change targets that gap: give the algorithm a way to recognize a dead-end branch early and skip it entirely. 
                            The greedy seed ensures pruning is active from the very first call rather than only after stumbling onto a complete tour organically.

                        4. Why do those changes make the algorithm faster?
                            The lower bound check is the dominant speedup — if the cheapest imaginable way to finish the current partial tour still can't beat the known best, 
                            the entire subtree rooted at that call is skipped in one step. 
                            The greedy seed makes this check aggressive immediately rather than gradually. 
                            Sorting neighbors so cheaper edges are explored first means good solutions are found earlier, 
                            which tightens ans sooner, which makes the lower bound check fire more often and cut more branches. 
                            The bitmask removes per-call allocation and turns visited checks into single CPU instructions. 
                            These effects compound: each pruned subtree prevents thousands or millions of recursive calls.
